Reviewer: 1

Comments to Author
The authors investigated which of several electroencephalography (EEG) measures best predicted states of covert attention engagement and corresponding behavioural task performance. The EEG measures tested were alpha amplitude, alpha-gamma cross frequency coupling and alpha functional connectivity between different electrodes. The results revealed that the measure which most strongly dissociated trials with covert attention from those without was alpha amplitude. However, no measure strongly predicted behavioural task performance.  

The authors suggest that the results largely support the recently proposed baseline sensory excitability model (BSEM) of alpha function.

The study employs state of the art analysis methods and he manuscript is well written. However, I believe some important methodological issues require clarification and I am not sure what exactly the ‘take home’ message of the study is. I have elaborated on my concerns below:

1. The authors acknowledge in the discussion that using EEG to measure gamma-band activity comes with limitations. It is also sub-optimal for functional connectivity metrics. This being the case, the choice of EEG to test the stated hypotheses is not optimal. I wonder whether the authors might be able to explicitly state, perhaps in the discussion, exactly what the current results can confidently add to the literature given these limitations? The fact that pre-stimulus alpha amplitude dissociates between cued and non-cued trials is certainly not a novel finding, and I have concerns about how much one should read into the CFC and connectivity results given the inherent limitations of using EEG to capture these measures (see further points below for details). The analysis approach is sophisticated, but I found myself struggling to grasp exactly what the study adds that wasn’t known already.

2. One issue that I wonder about is whether differences in the classification performance of the different EEG measures may be partly attributable to differences in their signal-to-noise ratios? For instance, alpha amplitude is likely a less noisy estimate which is easier to measure with EEG than cross frequency coupling metrics. Might differences in signal-to-noise represent a confounding factor for interpreting the results?

3. The task employed was quite different to tasks usually adopted by studies investigating the influence of prestimulus alpha on perception (i.e. usually simple hit/miss detection tasks of 2-AFC discrimination tasks). One potentially important difference is that the task employed here required participants to use the computer mouse to rotate a needle to reproduce the direction of the presented target. It seems that errors on this task will be influenced by more factors than just visual perceptual factors (i.e. motor errors will potentially also have a strong influence). Do the authors think that this might have contributed to the lack of EEG-behavioural effects that they found?

4. The following is from the Methods section regarding multiple comparison correction:

“For group level statistical analysis, t-tests using a threshold of p < 0.05 were used to
compare the empirical results with permuted-labels results (Bode et al. 2019). Subsequently, right-tailed tmax permutation testing (number of iterations = 10000, α level = 0.05) was conducted for multiple comparisons correction (Blair and Karniski 1993).”

My interpretation is that the multiple comparison correction was applied within each separate analysis (across timepoints?). However, given the number of different EEG metrics being tested (and for left and right targets separately etc.), are the authors confident that the type-1 error rate is being controlled to an acceptable degree?

5. There seems to be a visual field asymmetry in behavioural performance (Figure 2), with larger errors on the left than the right side. Could the authors comment on why this might be?


Reviewer: 2

Comments to Author
General

The general approach, implementation and results of this manuscript seem solid. However, the methods are not always described in a way that makes it clear precisely what was done. In a few spots, the writing is too colloquial. Further, some passages especially in the discussion are confusing and do not seem to reflect a thorough understanding of electrophysiology. The results nicely fit with the hypotheses. If possible, it might be nice to have a “summary” figure where the effectiveness of the different metrics in MVPA was compared. Figure 3-6 are hardly readable.

Abstract
- The listing of 3 different possible functions for alpha is too elaborate for the abstract
- Abbreviation CFC needs to be explained  in the abstract
- “pre-target” should perhaps be renamed “pre-stimulus” in the abstract
- Metrics cannot have activity, they can show time courses or patterns
- The terms “SVM” and “SVR” should be explained. Or maybe do not refer to these specific methods at all in the abstract.
- “alpha amplitude alone is most sensitive” – this sounds as if alpha amplitude alone was better than when combined with CFC and/or synchrony, which was not tested for.

Introduction:
- Background and own approach/hypothesis should be more separate in the introduction. Now the own approach is briefly mentioned on p4. l.50 and then the text goes back to describing at length different models for alpha, then to describe for each model how it will be tested in the study, then to next model… Would be good to have a clearer structure here.
- P6, l.117-9: this sentence should not end in a question mark.
- P6, l.122: “the idea is” is too colloqiual for a scientific manuscript

Methods:
- P8, l.164: what is meant by “identical to the fixation”?
- P9, l.176: “rest at their own pace” means “take a break for as long as they wanted”?
- P11, l 230: Please explain what is the difference between (relative?) “response error” and “absolute response error”?
- P11, l.233: please formulate better
- P13, l.263-5: how do you come to the conclusion that they “did not use covert attention as instructed”?
- P13, l.268ff: technically, the FOOOF only separates 1/f component from peak components, which does not necessarily equate to aperiodic and periodic components, respectively. However, for the purposes here (in alpha band which has a strong peak), this method is sufficient.
- P14, l.296-7: Are these individual alpha frequencies reported in the manuscript? Were they used in analyses?
- P15-16: How did you decide that other types of CFC (notably phase-phase coupling aka cross-frequency phase synchrony) are not relevant for the study? Also, please write that you only estimated local CFC (as opposed to inter-areal CFC). Please explain the filtering for the high frequency better. Did you extract a single signal for a whole frequency range? And was this 15-60 or 30-45 Hz? (the former would include beta band)

Results:
- Please avoid “As can be seen in…” formulations
- Figure 2 should be divided into A and B panel
- P25, l 550-7: Why is the response error mentioned first in text, but shown second in the figure?

Discussion:
- P29, l.628: Shouldn’t this be “in/during the 1000 ms before target onset”? The way it is written now, might be interpreted as “exactly 1000 ms before”
- P29, l.632: I recommend writing “which predicts that changes”
- P30, l.663-74: Which points are you trying to make here? You are comparing to Voytek’s finding of theta-high-gamma PAC involving frequency ranges that were not investigated in this study. Also, what is meant by surface EEG here? Why should it not be possible to record high-gamma from outside the head (which is what Bonnefond & Jensen 2015 did with MEG)?



Figures 3-6: Currently, these figures have considerably too low resolution, too small sub-panels, and too small font size to be readable. Some abbreviations or shortenings may be used in labels if they are explained in legend (e.g. using just MI instead of Modulation Index; using just Accuracy instead of Classification Accuracy). Time points could be given only every 200 ms, but in larger font.
